Dental Hygiene

The cleaning itself is thorough and, for most patients with healthy gums, comfortable. If it&#x27;s been a while since your last visit or there&#x27;s significant buildup present, we&#x27;ll let you know what to expect before we begin.

  • Polishing to remove surface stains and smooth the enamel
  • Flossing between every tooth to clear out anything left behind
  • A fluoride treatment, in some cases, to strengthen enamel and reduce cavity risk
  • A full exam to check for signs of decay, gum disease, or other concerns

How often you should come in

The standard recommendation is a professional cleaning every six months, and for most patients, that&#x27;s the right interval. Some patients benefit from more frequent visits — typically every three to four months — if they have a history of gum disease, are prone to heavy tartar buildup, or have other factors that increase their risk of oral health problems. Dr. Steven Holm and Dr. David Guzek will let you know which schedule makes the most sense for your specific situation.

Skipping appointments or stretching the interval tends to allow more buildup to accumulate, making each cleaning more involved and giving gum disease more time to progress quietly in the background.

What you do at home matters just as much

Professional cleanings do the heavy lifting twice a year, but what happens in between determines a lot. The daily habits you keep at home are what prevent plaque from reaching the point where it hardens into tartar in the first place.

A few things that make a significant difference:

None of these is complicated, but consistency is what makes them effective.

  • Brush twice a day for at least two minutes each time, using a soft-bristled brush and fluoride toothpaste.
  • Floss once daily, ideally before bed, to remove food and plaque from between teeth where your brush can&#x27;t reach.
  • Replace your toothbrush every three to four months, or sooner if the bristles are frayed.
  • Drink water throughout the day, particularly fluoridated tap water, to rinse away food particles and support enamel health.
  • Limit sugary and acidic foods and drinks, which feed the bacteria that produce cavity-causing acid.

Understanding gum health

Gum disease is one of the most common oral health conditions adults face, and it often develops without obvious symptoms in the early stages. Gingivitis, the earliest form, typically shows up as redness, puffiness, or bleeding when you brush or floss. It&#x27;s reversible with improved hygiene and professional cleaning, but if left unaddressed, it can progress to periodontitis, a more serious infection that damages the bone and tissue supporting the teeth.

We assess gum health at every visit by measuring the depth of the pockets between your gums and teeth. Shallow, healthy pockets indicate stable gum tissue. Deeper pockets suggest inflammation or disease that needs attention. Catching this early makes treatment far more manageable.

What happens when hygiene is neglected

The consequences of poor dental hygiene go beyond cavities. Gum disease has been linked to systemic health conditions, including heart disease, diabetes complications, and respiratory issues. The mouth is a gateway to the rest of the body, and chronic bacterial infection in the gums doesn&#x27;t stay entirely local.

From a purely dental standpoint, neglected hygiene leads to decay, tooth loss, and the need for restorative work that could have been avoided. Fillings, crowns, root canals, extractions, and implants are more likely outcomes when preventive care isn&#x27;t consistent.
