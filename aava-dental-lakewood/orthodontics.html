Common Issues Orthodontics Can Fix

Orthodontic treatment isn’t one-size-fits-all. It’s tailored to the unique needs of each patient, and those needs often fall into one of these categories:

Crowded Teeth

When there’s not enough space in the jaw, teeth compete for room—overlapping and twisting. Crowding makes brushing and flossing tricky, which means more plaque buildup and a greater chance of cavities and gum disease.

Open Bite

This is when the upper and lower teeth don’t meet properly when you bite down, often due to habits like thumb sucking or tongue thrusting. It can make chewing inefficient and affect speech clarity.

Deep Overbite

In this case, the upper front teeth excessively overlap the lower ones. Left untreated, it can wear down tooth enamel, cause jaw strain, and even result in gum trauma behind the upper front teeth.

Crossbite

A crossbite occurs when the upper teeth close behind the lower teeth, rather than overlapping them as they should. This misalignment can affect the front teeth, the back teeth, or both. Left untreated, it may lead to uneven tooth wear, gum recession, and a higher chance of chipping or damaging your teeth.

Increased Overjet

An excessive overjet—sometimes nicknamed “buck teeth”—is when the upper front teeth jut out too far ahead of the lower ones. This is often linked to habits like thumb sucking or chronic mouth breathing, and it leaves those front teeth more vulnerable to injury or accidental trauma.

Reverse Overjet

Also called an underbite, this is when the lower teeth jut out past the upper ones. It’s not just a cosmetic concern—it can lead to difficulty biting and chewing, and even affect facial symmetry.

Spacing Issues

Too much space between teeth—whether from missing teeth, small tooth size, or developmental factors—can create both functional and aesthetic challenges. Orthodontics helps close those gaps while aligning the bite.

Methods of Orthodontic Treatment

Orthodontists have more tools in their toolkit than ever before. Treatment plans can include:

Treatments are customized not only to the dental problem but also to age, oral health status, and lifestyle considerations. Children often start earlier to influence jaw growth, while adults might prioritize discreet options with minimal disruption.

  • Fixed braces : These are the classic metal brackets and wires many people are familiar with, though today’s versions are smaller, sleeker, and more comfortable.
  • Removable aligners : Clear aligners like Invisalign are a popular option for teens and adults seeking a more discreet experience.
  • Appliances for growth guidance : For younger patients, devices like palatal expanders help guide jaw development and create space before permanent teeth erupt.

Risks and Considerations in Orthodontic Treatment

Like any medical procedure, orthodontic treatment isn’t risk-free—but complications are rare when treatment is managed by a licensed orthodontist and supported with proper care.

Potential Risks

The key to reducing these risks? Consistent oral hygiene, regular check-ins, and following your orthodontist’s instructions to a T.

  • Tooth decay and gum problems : Braces and aligners can trap food particles and plaque. Without good hygiene, this may lead to cavities or gum inflammation.
  • Root resorption : In some cases, the roots of teeth can shorten slightly during movement. While usually not harmful, your orthodontist monitors this closely during treatment.
  • Gum support loss : In patients with pre-existing gum disease, movement of teeth must be carefully managed to avoid destabilizing the surrounding bone and tissue.
